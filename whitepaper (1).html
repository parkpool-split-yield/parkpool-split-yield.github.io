Parkpool

Parking lots as a tradable asset class: a stablecoin-funded pool buys, leases, and operates parking capacity — and distributes the proceeds back to its investors.

Draft, not final Concept study Chain-agnostic

01Summary

Parkpool is a draft concept for a fund that opens up parking lots as a real-world asset (RWA) to stablecoin investors. Instead of buying a single property, investors deposit stablecoins into a pool and receive a proportional claim on a shared portfolio of parking assets and parking-related trading strategies.

The pool pursues two parallel paths to generate returns: short-term arbitrage with existing parking rights (e.g. subletting unused capacity, dynamic pricing during demand spikes) and long-term acquisition of parking lots that are leased out and may appreciate over time. Profits from both strategies flow into a shared net return, distributed pro rata to holders of the pool's share token.

This document describes the mechanics, strategy, and risks of such a model as a basis for discussion. It is a concept paper, not an offering.

02The Problem

Parking is a strange asset class: enormous as a market, but poorly opened up to investment capital.

  • Capital-intensive, yet small in ticket size. A single parking lot often costs too much for individual investors, yet is too small to seriously interest institutional funds.
  • Underused capacity. Many lots sit partially empty during the day, at night, or seasonally — that idle time is rarely monetized systematically.
  • Illiquid ownership. Selling a parking lot can take months; there's essentially no liquid secondary market for fractional ownership.
  • Fragmented operators. Small, local operators rarely have the data or capital to price dynamically or scale beyond their region.

Tokenization doesn't automatically solve a parking lot's physical illiquidity — but it can make the capital side more liquid: many small investors instead of one buyer, a tradable share instead of a deed.

03Market Opportunity

The global parking lots and garages market is estimated at roughly $97.3 billion in 2026, projected to grow to about $112.5 billion by 2030. That's a large but structurally fragmented market — dominated by local and regional operators rather than a handful of major players.

At the same time, the market for tokenized real-world assets is growing quickly: on-chain RWA value (excluding stablecoins) grew from roughly $5.4 billion in early 2025 to over $19 billion by March 2026 — a growth rate of more than 250% in 15 months. That space is still dominated by treasuries and private credit; physical, cash-flow-generating niche assets like parking are essentially untouched.

The thesis: parking lots combine two traits that make them well suited to tokenization — reliable, measurable cash flows (tickets, monthly permits, event pricing) and a ticket size that's too large for individual investors and too small for institutional funds.

04How It Works

At its core, Parkpool is a vault contract that accepts stablecoin deposits and deploys them into real-world parking strategies. The loop runs in five steps:

The components

  • On-chain vault. A smart contract accepts stablecoins and mints PARK share tokens proportional to the pool's current net asset value (NAV) — comparable to a tokenized fund share.
  • Legal wrapper (SPV). Since parking lots are real, off-chain assets, a separate legal structure (Special Purpose Vehicle) holds the actual ownership or lease rights. The token represents an economic claim against that structure, not the deed itself.
  • Reporting and oracle layer. Occupancy data, revenue, and independently verified valuations of the lots are published on-chain periodically and update the NAV.
  • Redemption. Investors can redeem PARK shares for stablecoins at the current NAV, subject to a liquidity buffer and potential lock-ups on acquired real-estate positions.

05Investment Strategy

Capital in the pool is allocated between two strategies following a barbell logic: fast, liquid, and higher-yielding on one end; slow, asset-backed, and stable on the other.

short-term

Arbitrage

The pool trades existing parking rights without owning the lot:

  • Subletting unused monthly permits and reselling them at hourly rates
  • Dynamic pricing around events, holidays, and weather
  • Buying and reselling capacity blocks at airports, stadiums, and hospitals during peak periods

Short capital lock-up, fast turnaround — useful for building early returns and a track record.

long-term

Acquisition

The pool buys parking lots outright through the SPV:

  • Focus on underused lots in growing suburban and transit-adjacent locations
  • Stable income from monthly permit holders and operator contracts
  • Optional upside from repurposing, e.g. adding EV charging infrastructure

Higher capital lock-up, but backed by real assets and less exposed to short-term demand swings.

In early phases the pool weights capital more heavily toward arbitrage, to demonstrate capital efficiency and a proven track record. As pool size grows, allocation gradually shifts toward acquisitions.

06Token Structure

The PARK share token follows the basic principle of a tokenized fund share: its value is derived entirely from the underlying portfolio's NAV, not from speculation on the token itself.

Illustrative structure — not final parameters
ElementDescription
Deposit currencyEstablished stablecoins (e.g. USDC), potentially several in parallel
Share tokenPARK — represents a NAV-proportional claim on the pool
Valuation cadencePeriodic NAV update, backed by audited operating and valuation data
Legal anchorSPV per jurisdiction, holding ownership or usage rights to the lots

Two distribution modes are conceivable: a rebasing model, where profits automatically raise NAV per share, or a claim model, where investors actively withdraw periodic stablecoin payouts. Both can be offered in parallel, letting investors choose.

07Profit Distribution — Splityield

Splityield is the name of Parkpool's distribution engine: the fixed waterfall that turns arbitrage and leasing proceeds into a pro-rata payout per PARK share. Gross proceeds first flow into a shared revenue pool, then get distributed in this order:

Illustrative example per 100 units of gross revenue — not a return guarantee
Line itemAmount
Gross revenue (arbitrage + leasing)100.0
Operating costs (maintenance, insurance, management)−18.0
Management fee (example: 2% p.a., pro-rated)−2.0
Performance fee (example: 20% above hurdle rate)−8.0
Net proceeds to PARK holders72.0

Net proceeds are distributed strictly pro rata by shares held — no preferred classes unless explicitly structured otherwise. Actual cost ratios depend heavily on location, scale, and jurisdiction, and are kept deliberately illustrative here.

08Governance

Two decision layers are worth keeping separate:

  • Operational decisions — pricing, subletting, tactical arbitrage — run algorithmically and at the operating team's discretion, since they require speed.
  • Strategic decisions — larger acquisitions, new target markets, changes to the fee structure — go to an investor body for a vote, weighted by PARK shares held.

Independent checks — smart-contract audits, verification of the SPV's ownership documentation, periodic audits of occupancy and revenue data — are a prerequisite, not an afterthought, before the pool accepts capital at any meaningful scale.

09Risk Factors

This list is not exhaustive. A real-world venture of this kind would require a full legal and financial risk review in every relevant jurisdiction.
  • Regulatory risk. A share token with profit participation meets the definition of a security in most jurisdictions. Without proper licensing, a public offering of this kind is not permitted in many countries.
  • Legal enforceability risk. The link between an on-chain token and off-chain ownership via an SPV is legally complex and not equally enforceable everywhere in a dispute.
  • Liquidity risk. Physical parking lots are illiquid. Redemptions may need to be time-limited or gated during periods of high demand.
  • Market risk. Structural shifts — autonomous vehicles, more remote work, expanded public transit — can reduce long-term parking demand in specific cities.
  • Smart contract and oracle risk. Bugs in the vault code, or inaccurate or delayed reporting data, can misstate the NAV.
  • Concentration risk. Early phases with few locations are more exposed to local demand shocks than a diversified portfolio.

10Roadmap

  • Phase 1 — Pilot. One location, a small pool, manually supervised arbitrage to test the operating assumptions.
  • Phase 2 — Expansion. Multiple cities, a formalized SPV structure, first independent audit, and custodian integration.
  • Phase 3 — Secondary market. PARK shares become tradable on an RWA-capable secondary market; additional stablecoins are supported.
  • Phase 4 — Institutional scale. Opening to qualified institutional investors, possible expansion into adjacent asset classes like EV charging infrastructure.
